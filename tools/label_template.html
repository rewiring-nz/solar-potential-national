Three kinds of line

Seen from above, which is how you’ll be looking at them.
Ridge line
Any fold where the roof falls away on both sides. Flat along the top (a ridge) or running downhill to a corner (a hip) — both go here. They are the same thing to the model, so do not spend time deciding which. A roof shaped angle–flat–angle has several of these; mark them all.
Valley line
The opposite fold: the roof rises away on both sides, so rain would collect and run down it. You get one wherever two roof sections join, most often on an L or T shaped house.
Cliff line
Not a fold at all — the surface stops and the height jumps. Covers both a stepped or split-level roof and the sides of something raised: a dormer, clerestory, plant box or lift overrun.

How to draw it: put the line along the top edge of the higher surface, following the lip where it drops away. For a dormer, that means drawing round its sides — one line per side.
The quick test. Does the line sit at the top with roof falling away both sides? Ridge. Does it run downhill to a corner, with roof falling away both sides? Hip. Does it run downhill with roof rising away both sides, so water would sit in it? Valley. Does the surface simply stop and drop, like a little cliff? Cliff line.

There are only three because that is all the code actually uses: is this a fold (the surface carries on at a new angle) or a break (it stops and drops)? Ridge versus hip is architectural vocabulary the pipeline never consults, so it is not worth your time.

If you cannot tell whether a line is a fold or a break at all, leave it out rather than guess — a wrong label is worse than a missing one.
